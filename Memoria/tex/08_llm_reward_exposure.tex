\chapter[Learning Collision Avoidance from LLM-Derived Rewards]{Learning Collision Avoidance\\ from LLM-Derived Rewards}\label{ch:llmreward}

Chapter~\ref{ch:dependence} closed on an explicit open problem. Behavioural cloning of the
shield's steering removed shield \emph{dependence} (the weaned agent drives as well unaided as
it does shielded), but it left a residual \emph{collision} gap: without the shield the agent
still crashes on roughly one episode in six, because the shield's throttle is an emergency brake
and is therefore a poor longitudinal teacher (Section~\ref{sec:dep-collapse}). That chapter argued
the gap is ``better posed as a reward-shaping problem \dots\ that lets the agent learn its
\emph{own} gentle avoidance''. This chapter takes up that problem, and in doing so revisits, one
final time, the use of a Large Language Model (LLM) in the training loop, after the negative
result of Section~\ref{sec:dep-llm}.

The chapter is organised around a single thesis: the earlier LLM failure and the failure of a
hand-designed time-to-collision (TTC) penalty share \emph{one} root cause, that cause dictates the
design that ought to work, and, once the design is built and every confound is removed, it
produces a clean negative result that is itself informative. Section~\ref{sec:cov-diagnosis}
establishes the diagnosis; Section~\ref{sec:cov-method} derives the method (a dense reward
\emph{learned} from an LLM's preferences, delivered under a manufactured exposure curriculum);
Sections~\ref{sec:cov-reward}--\ref{sec:cov-shaping} detail its three components;
Section~\ref{sec:cov-perception} reports the perception wall that the first experiments ran into
and the systems fix that broke it; Section~\ref{sec:cov-results} reports the de-confounded
experiment; and Section~\ref{sec:cov-discussion} places the result in the
\emph{experience\,$>$\,gradient\,$>$\,incentive} hierarchy that organises the whole of
Chapters~\ref{ch:dependence}--\ref{ch:llmreward}.

\section{Diagnosis: one root cause behind two negative results}\label{sec:cov-diagnosis}

Two attempts to use a language model or a designed reward to close the avoidance gap failed, and,
on inspection, they failed identically.

\paragraph{The permissiveness dial (Section~\ref{sec:dep-llm}).} The LLM controlled a single scalar
every hundred episodes; its output was \emph{not causally coupled} to the metric it was meant to
move: the correlation between permissiveness and the shield-activation rate was
\(\approx 0\). A fixed schedule would have produced the same outcome at no cost.

\paragraph{The TTC penalty.} A hand-designed time-to-collision / following-distance penalty was
added to the reward shaper and its weight swept across several runs. The penalty \emph{fired in only
\(\approx 3\%\) of episodes}, its magnitude when it fired was flat across weights, and the
shield-off crash rate did not fall; it merely redistributed failures from off-road to
collision.

\paragraph{The common cause.} Both signals were \emph{starved}. On the Town04 highway loop with the
three-channel LIDAR of Chapter~\ref{ch:design}, a vehicle entered the ego's front cone closely
enough to register in only about \(3\%\) of episodes, so any collision-specific
signal, whether an LLM dial or a TTC penalty, almost never had anything to act on. Three
constraints follow, and the method of Section~\ref{sec:cov-method} is the minimal design that
satisfies all three:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Bandwidth.} The learning signal must be \emph{dense} (per step), like the
        behavioural-cloning term of Equation~\eqref{eq:bc}, not a per-window scalar.
  \item \textbf{Exposure.} A collision signal starves at \(3\%\) coverage; the relevant experience
        must be \emph{manufactured} (or otherwise made frequent).
  \item \textbf{Stationarity.} A live additive reward rescales the running return normaliser the
        critic is calibrated against (Section~\ref{sec:design-agent-ppo}); to avoid the
        park-attractor collapse of Section~\ref{sec:dep-collapse}, the signal must be
        \emph{policy-invariant}.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Method: a dense LLM-learned reward over generated exposure}\label{sec:cov-method}

The proposed method, which we call \emph{Shielded Motif under generated exposure}, has three
components that map one-to-one onto the three constraints. A \textbf{preference-trained reward
model} (Section~\ref{sec:cov-reward}) supplies a dense, per-step safety signal learned from a
language model's judgements, \emph{offline}, so the model never runs inside the control loop.
An \textbf{exposure director} (Section~\ref{sec:cov-exposure}) manufactures vehicle encounters,
supplying the missing experience. A \textbf{potential-based shaping} integration
(Section~\ref{sec:cov-shaping}) delivers the learned signal without altering the optimal policy.
Throughout, the safety shield and the deterministic safety governor of
Chapter~\ref{ch:dependence} are retained unchanged: the shield still prevents unsafe
\emph{exploration} on the harder, manufactured scenarios, and the ``proposer\,+\,deterministic
veto'' pattern, already shown to be sound independently of the LLM
(Section~\ref{sec:dep-discussion}), now guards the exposure curriculum.

The design follows the Motif paradigm of intrinsic motivation from artificial-intelligence
feedback~\cite{klissarov2023motif}, in which an LLM expresses preferences over pairs of observation
captions that are then distilled into a reward model; it inherits the preference-learning
formulation of~\cite{christiano2017preferences} and the policy-invariance guarantee
of~\cite{ng1999shaping}. It is related to, but distinct from, language-conditioned reward
generation for driving~\cite{huang2025vlmrl} and LLM-based safety-critical scenario
generation~\cite{zhang2024chatscene}, from which the exposure director borrows its
intent-conditioned design; unlike the latter line of work, here the language model shapes the
\emph{reward} rather than authoring the scenario script. The defining choice, and the one that directly answers the failure of
Section~\ref{sec:dep-llm}, is that the language model acts \emph{entirely offline}: it never
contends with CARLA for GPU memory and adds no latency to the \(20\,\mathrm{Hz}\) control loop.

\section{Component I: the preference-trained reward model}\label{sec:cov-reward}

\subsection{Offline preference annotation}

A dataset of \(739\)-dimensional observations was collected by rolling out the weaned policy of
Chapter~\ref{ch:dependence} under dense traffic and saving the observations the agent actually sees
(frozen observation normalisation, as at evaluation). Pairs of scenes were then sampled, with
in-path encounters over-sampled so that the annotator spends its budget where collisions are
decided, and each scene was rendered into a short natural-language caption describing the front
clearance, the nearest in-path vehicle, the lateral offset and the speed.

The first annotation attempt reused the local Gemma model from the abandoned shield tuner. It
failed in an instructive way: it abstained or contradicted itself on roughly \(85\%\) of pairs,
exhibited a strong position bias (it preferred whichever scene was presented first), and, when it
did commit, it tracked ``which car is driving more nicely'' rather than ``which car is closer to a
collision''. The annotation was therefore moved to a stronger model accessed through a batch of
parallel agents, with two changes that proved decisive: a \emph{strict, collision-only} prompt that
explicitly decouples ``stopped'' from ``in danger'', and \emph{symmetric} querying, whereby each pair is
annotated in both orderings and the two judgements are combined, which cancels position bias by
construction. The resulting labels passed a battery of semantic integrity gates (a closer in-path
vehicle is judged less safe; a larger lateral offset is judged less safe; the labels are internally
consistent under reordering) before any model was trained on them.

\subsection{The reward model}

The reward model \(r_\phi\) is a small multilayer perceptron
(\(250\rightarrow64\rightarrow64\rightarrow1\)) that reads a fixed slice of the observation (the
dynamic-LIDAR channel together with the lane and vehicle scalars), defined once as a single
source of truth shared with the shaping stage. It is trained from preferences with the
Bradley\textendash Terry objective~\cite{christiano2017preferences}: for an annotated pair of scenes
\((s_a, s_b)\) in which the language model judges \(s_a\) the safer situation,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bt}
  \mathcal{L}(\phi) \;=\; -\,\mathbb{E}_{(s_a,s_b)}\;
  c_{ab}\,\log \sigma\!\bigl(r_\phi(s_a) - r_\phi(s_b)\bigr),
\end{equation}
where \(\sigma\) is the logistic (sigmoid) function, \(r_\phi(s_a)-r_\phi(s_b)\) is the reward gap
between the two scenes and \(c_{ab}\in[0,1]\) is the language model's stated confidence, used to
down-weight uncertain pairs. The objective is the negative log-likelihood of the observed
preferences under the model \(P(s_a\succ s_b)=\sigma(r_\phi(s_a)-r_\phi(s_b))\)
(Figure~\ref{fig:bradley-terry}). The sign convention is that a \emph{higher} \(r_\phi\) denotes a
\emph{safer} situation. Unlike a crash-labelled safety critic, which sees only the sparse binary
outcome of an episode, \(r_\phi\) provides a graded signal on the many near-miss steps that never
end in a crash.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.78\textwidth]{imgs/bradley_terry}
  \caption{The Bradley\textendash Terry model maps a reward gap to a preference probability: the
           probability that scene \(s_a\) is the safer of a pair is
           \(\sigma(r_\phi(s_a)-r_\phi(s_b))\). Training maximises this probability on the pairs the
           language model labelled, weighting each pair by its confidence \(c_{ab}\), which pushes
           \(r_\phi\) higher for the safer scene of every pair without ever seeing a numeric label.}
  \label{fig:bradley-terry}
\end{figure}

Trained on the curated labels, \(r_\phi\) recovers a clean, monotone notion of longitudinal safety:
averaged over the held-out observations, its output rises from \(-15.1\) when the nearest in-path
vehicle is \(5\)\textendash\(10\,\mathrm{m}\) ahead to \(+13.7\) when the lane is clear beyond
\(30\,\mathrm{m}\) (Figure~\ref{fig:cov-reward}). The model never saw a distance label; it inferred
the ordering purely from the language model's pairwise judgements.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.66\textwidth]{imgs/motif_reward_field}
  \caption{The learned reward \(r_\phi\) as a function of the nearest in-path vehicle distance,
           averaged over held-out observations (error bars: one standard deviation within each
           bin). \(r_\phi\) increases monotonically with front clearance, confirming that the
           Bradley\textendash Terry distillation of the LLM's preferences recovered a coherent
           longitudinal-safety ordering.}
  \label{fig:cov-reward}
\end{figure}

\section{Component II: the exposure director}\label{sec:cov-exposure}

Globally spawned NPCs satisfy neither the exposure constraint (encounters are rare) nor a
controllable difficulty schedule. The exposure director injects \emph{route traffic}: a small number
of slow leads placed directly on the ego's lane, ahead of it, by walking the waypoint chain forward
along the ego's direction of travel. Two implementation subtleties from CARLA's synchronous model
had to be respected: the ego transform is only valid \emph{after} a world tick, so the spawn uses
the transform captured at spawn time rather than a stale pre-tick pose; and \texttt{waypoint.next()}
follows the OpenDRIVE topological direction rather than the vehicle heading, so the chain direction
is chosen by the sign of the dot product between the ego forward vector and the lane forward vector.
Lead speeds are specified in \emph{absolute} terms (\(20\)\textendash\(28\,\mathrm{km/h}\),
calibrated against the \(\sim\!34\,\mathrm{km/h}\) the ego sustains in traffic) so that a lead is
genuinely a slower obstacle to be negotiated, not a target that drives away. The whole mechanism is
gated by a dedicated governor with crash ceilings that \emph{cut} traffic on a breach, reusing the
asynchronous-runner and deterministic-governor machinery salvaged from the abandoned meta-controller
stack. Progress is tracked by an \emph{encounter rate}, the fraction of episodes in which a
vehicle enters the front cone closely enough to register.

\section{Component III: policy-invariant shaping}\label{sec:cov-shaping}

The learned reward is delivered as potential-based shaping~\cite{ng1999shaping}, using \(r_\phi\) as
the potential:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:potential}
  F_t \;=\; \gamma\,r_\phi(s_{t+1}) - r_\phi(s_t),
  \qquad
  r^{\mathrm{shaped}}_t \;=\; r_t \;+\; \beta\,F_t ,
\end{equation}
where the potential is the learned reward itself, \(\Phi(s)\equiv r_\phi(s)\); \(\gamma\) is the MDP
discount factor; \(F_t\) is the potential-difference shaping term; \(\beta\ge0\) is a fixed scalar
weight; \(r_t\) is the environment reward; and \(r^{\mathrm{shaped}}_t\) is the reward actually used
for the PPO update. The terminal potential \(\Phi(\text{terminal})=0\) is enforced on episode
termination. Because \(F_t\) is a difference of
potentials, the set of optimal policies is provably unchanged~\cite{ng1999shaping}: the term can only
\emph{re-time} credit toward the states that precede danger, it cannot create a new optimum. This is
the formal guarantee that the park-attractor of Section~\ref{sec:dep-collapse}, in which a direct
supervised gradient overpowered the reward and drove the policy to a stationary equilibrium, cannot
recur here, and it is why the coefficient \(\beta\) is simply frozen per run: policy invariance holds
for \emph{any} constant \(\beta\), so no annealing schedule is needed.

\paragraph{A training-stability prerequisite: the \texttt{log\_std} anchor.} Every arm of this
chapter warm-starts from the weaned checkpoint of Chapter~\ref{ch:dependence}. Warm-starting
re-exposed a latent failure of the policy parameterisation: the entropy bonus would, over a few
dozen updates, push the action-noise parameter \texttt{log\_std} up against its ceiling and pin it
there, freezing exploration noise high and re-triggering the turtle/stuck collapse documented in
Chapter~\ref{ch:design}. The fix is a light quadratic \emph{anchor} on the raw \texttt{log\_std}
parameter toward the pre-image of its initial value, added to the PPO loss. With the anchor active
the saturated fraction stayed at \(0\%\) and the effective standard deviation settled at
\(\sigma\approx0.22\) and annealed down as the policy converged, rather than locking high. The
anchor is a precondition for any of the reward experiments below to be interpretable: without it,
every warm-started run collapses for reasons that have nothing to do with the reward under test.

\section{The perception wall}\label{sec:cov-perception}

The first end-to-end runs of the full method were catastrophic: shield-off crash rates of
\(74\%\) and then \(51\%\), far worse than the \(16\%\) control. The cause was neither the reward nor
the exposure director but the \emph{perception range}. The three-channel semantic LIDAR resolves a
vehicle only at about \(12\,\mathrm{m}\); yet on clear road the weaned policy cruises near
\(65\,\mathrm{km/h}\,(\approx 18\,\mathrm{m/s})\). A lead detected at \(12\,\mathrm{m}\) while
closing at highway speed leaves well under one second to react, a rear-end that is
\emph{geometrically unavoidable}, not a failure of policy. The exposure signal of
Section~\ref{sec:cov-exposure} was being starved by the same constraint: a vehicle had to be almost
on top of the ego before it registered at all.

The fix is a perception upgrade rather than a learning change. Raising the LIDAR's vertical channel
count from \(3\) to \(16\) lifts the effective vehicle-detection range from \(\approx12\,\mathrm{m}\)
to \(\approx71\,\mathrm{m}\); crucially, this leaves the \(739\)-dimensional observation layout
\emph{unchanged} (the channels are reduced over the same \(240\) angular bins), so it does not break
checkpoint compatibility. Re-collecting the annotation set and retraining \(r_\phi\) at
\(16\) channels reproduced the same clean monotonic reward of Figure~\ref{fig:cov-reward}.

The encounter curves in Figure~\ref{fig:cov-encounter} carry a second, more humbling, finding. The
control run \texttt{base16} uses \emph{global traffic only} (no exposure director), yet its
encounter rate climbs to \(34.3\%\), essentially identical to the \(33.1\%\) of the full
\texttt{motif16} run with the director active. In other words, once perception reached
\(70\,\mathrm{m}\), the sixty global NPCs already supplied ample encounters, and the manufactured
route traffic added nothing measurable. The exposure \emph{diagnosis} of
Section~\ref{sec:cov-diagnosis} was correct (the \(3\%\) ceiling was real), but the effective
\emph{cure} turned out to be the perception upgrade, not the traffic director. This is exactly the
kind of confound that an in-loop experiment hides and an isolated one exposes.

\section{Experiments}\label{sec:cov-results}

With perception fixed and the anchor in place, the question reduces to a single clean comparison:
does the LLM-learned preference reward reduce the shield-off collision gap? Two
\(16\)-channel runs, each warm-started from the weaned checkpoint and trained to a \(60\)-NPC
curriculum, isolate exactly that factor:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \texttt{base16}, the control: \(16\)-channel perception, global traffic, \emph{no}
        preference reward and \emph{no} exposure director.
  \item \texttt{motif16}, the full method: the same, \emph{plus} the preference reward of
        Equation~\eqref{eq:potential} and the exposure director.
\end{itemize}
Both were evaluated deterministically over \(50\) seed-aligned episodes at \(60\) NPCs, shield
removed and shield active, exactly as in Chapter~\ref{ch:dependence}.

The headline is a \textbf{null result}. With the shield off, the combined crash\,+\,off-road rate is
\(34\%\) for the method against \(36\%\) for the control, a difference of two points on fifty
episodes (\(z\approx0.76\), \(p\approx0.45\)), well inside sampling noise. The reward did move the
\emph{composition} of failures, and in a direction that is mechanistically informative: crashes rose
from \(16\%\) to \(22\%\) while off-road events fell from \(20\%\) to \(12\%\).

  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{imgs/motif_ablation}
  \caption{Outcome distribution with the shield off (left) and on (right), control versus method.
           The preference reward trades off-road events for collisions; the combined unsafe rate is

  \label{fig:cov-ablation}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Mechanism: lane-centring buys collisions.} A focused diagnostic over sixteen
seed-aligned shield-off episodes explains the trade. The method drives much closer to the lane
centre: mean absolute lateral offset \(0.040\) against the control's \(0.095\), a factor of
\(2.4\). This is precisely what a reward that rewards ``safer'' lateral positioning should do, and it
is why off-road events fall. But it has a cost: of the control's three off-road episodes, two were
\emph{collision escapes} in which the agent swerved off the road to avoid a lead. By pinning the car
in its lane, the preference reward removes that escape route; when a lead then appears too late, the
agent rear-ends it instead of leaving the road. Three of the method's four diagnostic crashes are
front rear-ends with the nearest vehicle at \(2.3\)\textendash\(2.5\,\mathrm{m}\) and the ego still
travelling at \(17\)\textendash\(45\,\mathrm{km/h}\). The reward faithfully learned lateral safety, which
dominated the captions, but it carries no \emph{longitudinal} authority: it cannot
make the agent brake.

\paragraph{The residual is longitudinal control, and it is now detectable.} With perception fixed,
the agent demonstrably \emph{sees} the lead at \(70\,\mathrm{m}\); what it lacks is the policy to
brake hard enough in time. The shield-on rows of Table~\ref{tab:cov-ablation} confirm the hazard is
both detectable and avoidable (the shield drives the crash rate down to \(6\)\textendash\(10\%\)
by braking on exactly these encounters), so the gap is an execution gap in the learned policy,
not a perception or an unavoidability problem as it was before the perception fix. (The reward does
not interact harmfully with the shield: shielded success is identical at \(70\%\); the method's
slightly higher shielded crash mirrors its slightly lower stuck rate, as the in-lane bias also
reduces the parked deadlocks discussed in Chapter~\ref{ch:design}.)

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:cov-discussion}

This chapter completes an argument that runs across Chapters~\ref{ch:dependence}
and~\ref{ch:llmreward} and can be stated as a hierarchy: \emph{experience\,$>$\,gradient\,$>$\,
incentive}. The TTC penalty supplied an \emph{incentive} but no experience to apply it to, and
failed. Behavioural cloning supplied a \emph{gradient} on the shielded states and fixed steering,
but the shield's brake-only throttle made it a poor longitudinal teacher, so a gap remained. This
chapter supplied the missing \emph{experience} (first by manufacturing it, then, more decisively,
by extending perception so that ordinary traffic became experience) and a dense, policy-invariant,
LLM-learned \emph{incentive} on top of it. The incentive then did exactly what its training signal
encoded (it improved lateral safety), but it could not, on its own, install the longitudinal
braking skill, because braking was under-represented in the captions the language model ranked.

The result is therefore a \emph{clean, bounded, and de-confounded negative result}, and that is its
value. Every alternative explanation for the earlier failures was removed in turn: position bias
(symmetric annotation with a stronger model), reward non-stationarity (potential-based shaping),
exploration collapse (the \texttt{log\_std} anchor), and, decisively, exposure
starvation (the perception upgrade, which alone lifted encounter coverage roughly tenfold). With all
of these controlled, a preference reward that steers toward ``safer'' scenes shifts the failure
distribution from off-road to collision without a net safety gain. The honest reading is not that
LLM-derived rewards cannot help, but that \emph{what the LLM is asked to rank determines what the
agent learns}: captions rich in lateral geometry and poor in closing dynamics yield a reward rich in
lane-keeping and poor in braking.

Two corollaries are worth recording for the project as a whole. First, the abandoned
meta-controller stack was not wasted: its schema-constrained decoding, deterministic governor and
asynchronous runner were repurposed as trustworthy \emph{offline} annotation and exposure
infrastructure. The negative result of Chapter~\ref{ch:dependence} is what \emph{forced} the
offline, policy-invariant design, rather than a lucky guess. Second, the perception wall is a
reminder that in an embodied agent a learning signal is only as good as the perception that feeds
it; a substantial share of what looked like a reward-design problem was in fact a sensor-range
problem.

The clearest next step follows directly from the mechanism. The reward should be made to encode the
quantity it currently misses: captioning the closing speed or time-to-collision explicitly, rather
than only the static front clearance, so that the Bradley\textendash Terry distillation can learn a
longitudinal ordering as cleanly as it learned the lateral one in Figure~\ref{fig:cov-reward};
alternatively, pairing the learned reward with an explicit longitudinal-control prior would supply
the braking authority the incentive alone cannot. A multi-seed replication would also tighten the
confidence intervals around the two-point difference reported here. These directions are taken up in
Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusions}.
